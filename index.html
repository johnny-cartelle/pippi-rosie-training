Brief summary

  • Prevent competition. Separate meals and valuable chews. Give each dog a resting spot and predictable turns for attention.
  • Reward calm before trouble starts. Notice good behavior early. Keep everyone’s responses consistent so pushing and pestering don’t keep paying off.
  • Use everyday routines. Your dinner → mat. Door opening → wait. Pair the situation with a familiar cue, reward it, then gradually fade the words.
  • Keep it easy. Train for 5–10 minutes; keep trigger practice to 1–5. Practice settling daily, shortening difficult steps. Pippi can sit or stand. Aim for relaxation, not just staying still.
  • Notice, then check in. Work far enough from a trigger that the dog can eat and respond. First mark and reward noticing it; later reward voluntarily looking back at you. Add distance if they get stuck staring.

For Jane at Waginn: down, go to mat, and gradually longer stays. Continue the previous exercises and add new ones a little at a time.

Who controls the resources

Fair, predictable access to food, chews, resting spots and attention helps prevent competition. Use separate spaces and reward calm behavior; give the dogs time apart during rebuilding.

Your dog is always learning

Everyday consequences teach habits. Prevent unwanted practice and reward an alternative before the problem starts. For a tricky pattern, record what happened before, what the dog did, and what happened next.

What your dog is telling you

Pestering can reflect what has worked before, including occasional attention or scolding. Look at the pattern and the dog’s needs, then make a consistent plan.

Open original handout · Word
Situations as cues

Make ordinary events predict useful behaviors: your dinner → mat, a door opening → wait. Present the situation before a familiar verbal cue, reward, and gradually fade the cue.

Open original handout · Word
Engage–disengage

At a comfortable distance, mark looking at the trigger and feed when the dog turns back. Later, mark the voluntary turn back instead. If the dog cannot eat or respond, increase distance.

Reducing leash reactivity

The same game with progression criteria: aim for 3–5 successful repetitions before advancing. If the dog fixates for over five seconds in phase two, return to phase one. Keep sessions to 1–5 minutes and move closer only when comfortable.

Protocol for relaxation

Reward relaxation while gradually adding movement and distractions. The “15 days” are stages, not a deadline. Repeat stages as needed and use Tessa’s modifications for Pippi and Rosie.
